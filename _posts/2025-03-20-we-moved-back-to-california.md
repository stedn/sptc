Big announcement: we've moved back to California, the solarpunk state, as we've taken to calling it half joking and half completely serious.

After a few years enjoying the lakes and prairies of Wisconsin and building real community in Madison, we've headed back to the Bay Area, a state with strong solarpunk ideals and a much easier place to live car-free. It's a decision we thought about for a long time, packed and unpacked in our heads more times than we can count, and it's not really a story of "Wisconsin bad, California good." It's more complicated than that, so we wanted to actually write it out instead of leaving it as a two line announcement.

The move itself was its own small adventure, and admittedly not our most solarpunk one. We packed a shipping pod for the stuff that couldn't come with us any other way, and within about a day it had been quietly taken over by bikes and ebike parts, to the point where we started joking that a recumbent bike with a fiberglass shell is honestly more useful than a couch anyway, so why not let it win the space. We drove the rest of the way out, stopping to see an old friend from the New Mexico leg of our original cross-country trip years earlier, and swinging through the Grand Canyon along the way. We felt a little guilty taking a car for it, but we used the drive to scout out whether the Grand Canyon might actually be reachable by public transit on some future trip, which felt like the least we could do to make peace with the contradiction.

## What we'll miss

Madison gave us more than we expected when we first moved there. We built a real community: the kind that shows up to a bike camping trip on a random Tuesday announcement, that lets you cut their old boat trailer down to a bike hitch in their driveway with an angle grinder, that keeps inviting you back to Blue Mounds year after year until it stops being a "someday" trip and just becomes a tradition nobody has to plan anymore.

Some of our favorite Midwest memories weren't even the trips we hosted for others. There was a weekend in Sparta, which bills itself, not without some justification, as the bicycling capital of the world. The highlight was biking through the converted train tunnels, one of them three quarters of a mile long and genuinely a little scary in the pitch dark with water dripping somewhere you couldn't see, which made it the perfect activity for spooky season. Sparta itself had a lot of fun bike kitsch too, including what might be the longest tandem bike either of us has ever laid eyes on, propped up somewhere downtown like a monument to a very specific kind of enthusiasm.

{% include carousel.html images="assets/images/2025/DBJnfMDtJhR_2.jpg,assets/images/2025/DBJnfMDtJhR_3.jpg" alt="Sparta bike tunnels" %}

And there was a proper winter backpacking overnight on the Superior Hiking Trail, in Tettegouche State Park, over the holiday break one year, our first real winter overnight. We'll admit it wasn't our most solarpunk trip on paper, since we carpooled with friends to get there, 60 miles out from Duluth, rather than reaching it under our own power. But it gave us a local, remote backpacking trip just one state away, and the payoff was worth the asterisk. The trail runs high along cliffs overlooking Lake Superior, and we got lucky with a soft, steady snowfall that made the whole thing feel like Big Sur if it could somehow get snowed in, all dramatic coastline and quiet white drifts at the same time. Not the easiest trip we've ever done, but exactly the kind of thing that made the long Midwest winters feel worth it.

None of that is small. We don't want this post to read like we're dismissing four years of a place that genuinely shaped how we travel and who we travel with.

## Why California, though

At the same time, the pull back to California wasn't just nostalgia for the coast or an excuse to complain about winter again. So much of what we care about, sustainable transit, bike infrastructure, climate policy that actually treats the problem like it's real instead of a talking point, is just easier to live inside of here than it was in Wisconsin. We didn't want to keep fighting our own state's infrastructure just to live the way we already believe in, spending our energy working around a system instead of just living inside one that mostly agrees with us. California isn't perfect, not even close, and we're not naive enough to pretend otherwise. But its policies point in the direction we're trying to walk, and that matters more than we expected it to before we actually lived somewhere that didn't.

So: new state, same values, and a backlog of Midwest memories we're genuinely grateful for. On to the next chapter, wherever the trains and buses decide to take us next.
